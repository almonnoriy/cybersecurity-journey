## 1. Introduction

Cybersecurity focuses on protecting information systems, networks, applications, and digital assets from security threats. An important part of this process is understanding the threats that may affect an organisation, the vulnerabilities that could expose its systems, and the risks associated with potential security incidents.

Threats, vulnerabilities, and risks are closely related concepts in information security. Understanding their differences helps organisations identify security weaknesses, evaluate potential consequences, and implement appropriate security controls.

## 2. Cybersecurity Threats

### Definition

A cybersecurity threat is a potential event, action, or circumstance that could cause harm to an information system, network, device, or organisation.

Threats may originate from malicious individuals, accidental human activities, natural disasters, or technical failures.

### Types of Cybersecurity Threats

**2.1 Human Threats**

Human threats originate from the actions of individuals, whether intentional or accidental.

Examples include:

- Phishing attacks.
- Social engineering.
- Insider threats.
- Unauthorised access attempts.
- Accidental deletion of important information.
- Disclosure of confidential information.

**2.2 Natural Threats**

Natural threats result from environmental events that can damage information systems and infrastructure.

Examples include:

- Floods.
- Earthquakes.
- Lightning.
- Severe storms.
- Natural fires.

**2.3 Technical and Environmental Threats**

These threats arise from equipment failures, infrastructure problems, or environmental conditions that affect system operations.

Examples include:

- Hardware failure.
- Power outages.
- Network interruptions.
- Server overheating.
- Cooling system failures.

### Threat Sources

Threat sources are individuals, groups, events, or circumstances capable of causing security incidents.

Examples include:

- Cybercriminals.
- Malicious insiders.
- Careless employees.
- Organised criminal groups.
- Natural events.

## 3. Cybersecurity Vulnerabilities

### Definition

A vulnerability is a weakness or flaw in a system, application, network, configuration, process, or security practice that could be exploited by a threat or contribute to a security incident.

Vulnerabilities can exist in software, hardware, network infrastructure, physical environments, and human practices.

### Types of Vulnerabilities

**3.1 Software Vulnerabilities**

Weaknesses in software applications or operating systems that may affect their security.

Examples:

- Unpatched software.
- Programming errors.
- Insecure application design.
- Improper input validation.

**3.2 Network Vulnerabilities**

Weaknesses in network infrastructure, configurations, or security mechanisms.

Examples:

- Insecure network configurations.
- Unnecessary open services.
- Weak network access controls.
- Inadequate network segmentation.

**3.3 Human Vulnerabilities**

Weaknesses resulting from human behaviour, insufficient knowledge, or poor security practices.

Examples:

- Weak password practices.
- Lack of cybersecurity awareness.
- Falling for phishing attempts.
- Sharing login credentials.

**3.4 Physical Vulnerabilities**

Weaknesses in the physical protection of information systems and equipment.

Examples:

- Unlocked server rooms.
- Unsecured computer equipment.
- Poor physical access controls.
- Inadequate environmental protection.

**3.5 Configuration Vulnerabilities**

These occur when systems, applications, or devices are configured insecurely.

Examples:

- Default credentials that have not been changed.
- Excessive user permissions.
- Unnecessary services enabled.
- Incorrect file permissions.

## 4. Cybersecurity Risk

### Definition

Cybersecurity risk is the possibility of loss, damage, or disruption resulting from a security-related event, such as a threat exploiting a vulnerability.

Risk assessment helps organisations determine how likely a security incident is to occur and how serious its consequences could be.

### Risk Assessment Factors

Two important factors are considered when evaluating risk:

**Likelihood:** The probability that a particular security event will occur.

**Impact:** The potential consequences of the event on an organisation, its information, operations, or reputation.

A simplified risk calculation is:

\[
\text{Risk} = \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Impact}
\]

This model helps organisations estimate and prioritise risks. Risk assessment methods may use qualitative scales, quantitative measurements, or a combination of both.

### Example

A university stores students' personal information in an online database. If the database has weak access controls, an unauthorised individual may gain access to confidential records.

- **Threat:** Unauthorised access attempt.
- **Vulnerability:** Weak access controls.
- **Risk:** Exposure of confidential student information.
- **Potential impact:** Privacy violations, reputational damage, and financial or legal consequences.

## 5. Relationship Between Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Risks

Threats, vulnerabilities, and risks are interconnected concepts in cybersecurity.

A threat represents a potential source of harm, while a vulnerability represents a weakness that may make a system susceptible to that threat. Risk describes the potential for loss or damage arising from a security event.

| Concept | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Threat | A potential source of harm | An unauthorised access attempt |
| Vulnerability | A weakness that may be exploited | Weak authentication controls |
| Risk | The possibility of loss or damage | Exposure of confidential information |

Understanding this relationship allows cybersecurity professionals to identify weaknesses, evaluate potential consequences, and determine appropriate security measures.

## 6. Risk Management

### Definition

Risk management is the continuous process of identifying, assessing, prioritising, treating, and monitoring risks that could affect an organisation's information systems and operations.

Its purpose is to reduce risks to an acceptable level through appropriate security measures.

### Stages of Risk Management

**6.1 Risk Identification**

Identifying potential risks, threats, vulnerabilities, and assets that require protection.

**6.2 Risk Assessment**

Evaluating identified risks by considering their likelihood and potential impact.

**6.3 Risk Analysis and Prioritisation**

Determining the significance of each risk and deciding which risks require attention first.

**6.4 Risk Treatment**

Selecting and implementing appropriate measures to address identified risks.

**6.5 Risk Monitoring and Review**

Regularly reviewing risks, security controls, and organisational changes to ensure that risk management remains effective.

## 7. Risk Treatment Strategies

Organisations commonly use four major risk treatment strategies.

**7.1 Risk Mitigation**

Risk mitigation involves implementing security measures to reduce the likelihood or impact of a risk.

Example: Implementing Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) to strengthen account security.

**7.2 Risk Avoidance**

Risk avoidance involves discontinuing or preventing an activity that creates an unacceptable risk.

Example: Discontinuing the use of an insecure application that exposes sensitive organisational information.

**7.3 Risk Transfer**

Risk transfer involves shifting some of the financial consequences of a risk to another party.

Example: Purchasing cybersecurity insurance to help cover certain financial losses resulting from security incidents.

**7.4 Risk Acceptance**

Risk acceptance involves formally acknowledging a risk and deciding to retain it when its level falls within the organisation's acceptable limits.

Example: Accepting a low-impact risk when the cost of implementing additional controls outweighs the expected benefit.

## 8. Practical Application in a University Environment

Consider a university that uses an online academic management system to store student records, examination results, and staff information.

A security assessment identifies that some staff members use weak passwords.

The situation can be analysed as follows:

| Security Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Asset | Academic management system |
| Threat | Unauthorised account access |
| Vulnerability | Weak password practices |
| Risk | Exposure or unauthorised modification of academic records |
| Potential Impact | Privacy violations, academic disruption, and reputational damage |
| Recommended Controls | Multi-Factor Authentication, strong password policies, access controls, and security awareness training |

This example demonstrates how identifying threats and vulnerabilities helps organisations assess risks and select appropriate security controls.

| Risk | The possibility of loss or damage resulting from a security event. |
| Likelihood | The probability that an event will occur. |
| Impact | The potential consequences of an event. |
| Risk Assessment | The process of evaluating identified risks. |
| Risk Mitigation | Measures taken to reduce risk. |
| Risk Avoidance | Eliminating an activity that creates an unacceptable risk. |

| Security Control | A safeguard used to protect information and information systems. |

## 10. Conclusion

Threats, vulnerabilities, and risks are fundamental concepts in cybersecurity. They help organisations understand potential sources of harm, identify weaknesses in their information systems, and evaluate the consequences of possible security incidents.

Effective risk assessment and risk management enable organisations to prioritise security concerns, implement appropriate controls, and strengthen the protection of their information assets.

Understanding these concepts provides a foundation for further studies in vulnerability assessment, penetration testing, security operations, and information security management.
